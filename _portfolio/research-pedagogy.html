Research Pedagogy

I consider myself predominantly a biological oceanographer, wearing an extremely large hat that encompasses phytoplankton and zooplankton ecology in the context of ocean biogeochemical processes. For my sub-field expertise, I am particularly interested in describing gelatinous zooplankton ecological function, imaging methods across size spectra, and empirical field methods that quantify both abundances and rate-processes. I utilize cutting-edge technological approaches to collect in situ observational data, process the collected big-data at ~TB scale, and answer my research questions. I research the topics I do because I understand that there is little knowledge about some topics that fascinate me, and I want to structure research to answer these gaps.

Modern plankton ecology is built upon in situ methods and big-data scale repositories. The advancement of the field has spurred a new need for the traditional microscope-wielded plankton ecologist to develop computational abilities that sometimes rival our peers with wonky genetics data. I am personally of the opinion to make the best of every situation, and that this new plethora of data and computational abilities enables plankton ecologists to re-examine many fundamental assumptions of spatial distributions, patchiness, and other aspects of Redfield’s “paradox of the plankton”. Now, more than ever, the plankton ecologist might be able to spend less time at a microscope estimating abundances and more time integrating physical or chemical oceanography to understand how processes link across scales.
